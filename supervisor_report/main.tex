\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
\thispagestyle{plain}

Just-in-time software defect prediction (JIT-SDP) flags risky commits at the
moment they are made. Almost every JIT-SDP dataset takes its labels from the
SZZ algorithm, which infers defect-introducing commits by tracing the lines
changed in a bug fix back through version history. That inference fails when
bug-fixing commits are tangled with unrelated edits, and because models are
normally trained \emph{and} evaluated on SZZ labels, the resulting errors are
invisible to standard evaluation.

This thesis measures what SZZ-induced label noise costs a defect predictor
under realistic deployment conditions. This report covers the first two of its
four phases, on 27,319 commits from 21 Apache Java projects (JIT-Defects4J),
using an independently constructed reference label set that is held out and
used only for scoring.

\textbf{Phase~1} compares six SZZ variants with the reference labels. No
variant exceeds 27.2\% precision; the noise is class-conditional rather than
symmetric (false-alarm rates 6.7--26.3\%, miss rates 35.9--73.3\%); and the
variants agree with each other (Cohen's $\kappa$ up to 0.933) far more than
with the reference ($\kappa$ 0.158--0.202). \textbf{Phase~2} trains three
models on seven label sources under four evaluation regimes (16,380 runs,
paired project-level tests with Holm correction). Random $k$-fold
cross-validation inflates a high-capacity model by 0.137 MCC, and scoring it
against its own training labels adds a further 0.230. Under streaming
evaluation with verification latency, training on the reference labels beats
every SZZ variant by 0.034--0.064 MCC. A result of 0.41 MCC under the common
protocol falls to 0.10 under a realistic one. The remaining two phases will
identify the mechanism behind the damage and test a noise-aware online
learner.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Background and motivation}

Software defects become more expensive the longer they survive. \emph{Defect
prediction} directs limited review and testing effort towards the code most
likely to be faulty, and \emph{just-in-time software defect prediction}
(JIT-SDP) makes that prediction for each commit as it arrives, so that a risky
change can be reviewed while its author still remembers it~\cite{kamei2013jit}.
The setting has three defining properties: commits arrive as a
\textbf{stream}, so evaluation must respect time; defect-introducing commits
are \textbf{rare} (8.54\% in our corpus); and \textbf{a commit's label is
unknown when it arrives} --- it becomes known only once the defect is found and
fixed.

Projects record which commits \emph{fix} defects, not which introduced them.
The SZZ algorithm~\cite{sliwerski2005szz} bridges this gap by applying
\texttt{git blame} to the lines changed in a bug fix and labelling the commits
that last touched those lines as defect-introducing. Its premise --- that the
lines a fix changes are the lines that were wrong --- often fails, because
fixes are \emph{tangled} with refactoring, renaming and formatting: Herbold et
al.~\cite{herbold2022tangling} found that only 17--32\% of the changes in
bug-fixing commits address the bug itself. Since JIT-SDP models are usually
both trained and evaluated on SZZ labels, such errors cannot be seen from
inside the evaluation.

Evaluation adds a second problem. Random $k$-fold cross-validation, still
common, lets a model train on future commits~\cite{falessi2020order}. Even a
chronological split assumes that training labels are available immediately; in
our corpus the median delay between a commit and the fix that reveals its
defect is 113 days. Cabral and Minku~\cite{cabral2019orb} called this
\emph{verification latency} and proposed ORB, the online learner used in this
work.

\subsection{Problem statement}

\begin{tcolorbox}[probbox]
JIT-SDP models are trained and evaluated almost exclusively on labels produced
by SZZ, using protocols that ignore commit order and verification latency. The
field therefore has no measurement of what SZZ-induced label noise costs a
defect predictor under realistic deployment conditions, and no way to separate
a model's ability to find defects from its ability to reproduce the heuristic
that labelled its training data.
\end{tcolorbox}

Addressing this requires separating four things: (i)~the noise in the labels;
(ii)~the inflation contributed by the evaluation protocol; (iii)~the mechanism
by which noise damages an online learner; and (iv)~whether a learner can be
made robust to it. \textbf{This report covers (i) and (ii)}, which are
Phases~1 and~2 of the thesis; (iii) and (iv) are planned work
(Section~\ref{sec:planned}).

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.1cm,labelwidth=0.9cm,itemsep=2pt]
\item[RQ1] How much do SZZ variants disagree with one another and with an
independently constructed reference, and what is the direction of each
variant's labelling bias? \emph{(Phase~1, this report.)}
\item[RQ2] How does the choice of SZZ label source shift measured JIT-SDP
performance across naive, chronological and prequential (streaming, with
latency) evaluation regimes? \emph{(Phase~2, this report.)}
\item[RQ3] Through what mechanism does label noise degrade an online learner,
and which of SZZ's two error types --- false positives or false negatives ---
does the damage? \emph{(Phase~3, planned.)}
\item[RQ4] Can an online learner recover performance lost to SZZ-origin noise
without clean labels, and without losing performance when labels are clean?
\emph{(Phase~4, planned.)}
\end{description}

\subsection{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.},leftmargin=1.1cm]
\item Characterise the label noise of six SZZ variants against an independent
reference, as class-conditional flip rates. \emph{(Completed.)}
\item Quantify how much the evaluation protocol --- temporal leakage and
scoring against the training labels --- inflates measured performance, and
separate this from the effect of the label source. \emph{(Completed.)}
\item Measure the cost of SZZ labels for an online learner evaluated as a
stream with realistic verification latency. \emph{(Completed.)}
\item Identify which SZZ error type causes the damage, and by what mechanism.
\emph{(Planned, Phase~3.)}
\item Design and evaluate a noise-aware online learner under a pre-registered
protocol. \emph{(Planned, Phase~4.)}
\end{enumerate}

related work, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset,
Section~\ref{sec:method} the methodology, Section~\ref{sec:results} the
results of Phases~1 and~2, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes and
outlines the remaining work.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Just-in-time defect prediction}

Kamei et al.~\cite{kamei2013jit} established the modern form of JIT-SDP,
defining 14 change-level metrics in five families (Table~\ref{tab:kamei}) and
reporting about 68\% accuracy and 64\% recall on defect-inducing changes. A
systematic survey by Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2023survey} catalogues 67 JIT-SDP
studies built largely on this formulation. Because defect-introducing commits
are a small minority, accuracy is uninformative --- predicting ``clean''
everywhere scores 91.5\% on our corpus --- and oversampling of the minority
class is standard practice.

\subsection{The SZZ algorithm and its variants}

SZZ~\cite{sliwerski2005szz} extracts the lines modified by a bug fix, applies
\texttt{git blame} to find the last commit that touched each of them, and
labels those commits defect-introducing. Each of its two premises has a known
failure mode: \emph{tangled commits}, in which unrelated lines of a fix are
blamed as well~\cite{herbold2022tangling}, and \emph{blame attribution error},
in which the last commit to touch a line is not the one that made it wrong.
Later variants (Table~\ref{tab:variants}) add filters to remove the original
algorithm's over-collection.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{SZZ variants studied in this work.}
\label{tab:variants}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
Variant & Mechanism \\
\midrule
B-SZZ~\cite{sliwerski2005szz} & The original: blame every line modified by the fix \\
AG-SZZ~\cite{kim2006agszz} & Annotation graphs; filters cosmetic changes such as blank lines and comments \\
MA-SZZ~\cite{dacosta2017maszz} & Also excludes meta-changes such as branch merges and property changes \\
R-SZZ~\cite{davies2014rlszz} & Keeps only the most recent candidate commit \\
L-SZZ~\cite{davies2014rlszz} & Keeps only the candidate with the most changed lines \\
RA-SZZ~\cite{neto2018raszz} & Refactoring-aware; removes candidates caused by refactorings (Java only) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Evaluating SZZ needs a reference that does not come from SZZ. Rosa et
al.~\cite{rosa2021oracle,rosa2023szzvariants} built a \emph{developer-informed
oracle} from commit messages that explicitly name the bug-introducing commit
and found R-SZZ best (precision $\approx$ 66\%). That oracle, however,
contains no clean commits, so it cannot train a JIT-SDP model, and it scores
SZZ only on fixes already known to have an inducing commit, which is a
different question from labelling every commit in a project's history.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology}

Falessi et al.~\cite{falessi2020order} showed that validation schemes which do
not preserve the time order of data give misleading estimates for defect
classifiers, yet random $k$-fold remains common in JIT-SDP. For streams the
standard protocol is \emph{prequential} (test-then-train) evaluation; Gama et
al.~\cite{gama2013prequential} show that prequential \emph{error} with fading
factors converges to the Bayes error, although this guarantee does not extend
directly to non-decomposable metrics such as MCC. Cabral and
Minku~\cite{cabral2019orb} identified \emph{verification latency} as a
first-class concern: a clean commit is only ever presumed clean, while a
defective one must be discovered. Their later work~\cite{cabral2023reliable}
reports discovery delays from one day to over eleven years, with medians
typically at or below 90 days, which is the basis for our 90-day window.

\subsection{Online learning for JIT-SDP}

Online Bagging~\cite{oza2001onlinebagging} presents each arriving example to
each ensemble member $k \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ times. Oversampling
Online Bagging~\cite{wang2015oob} sets $\lambda$ from the running class ratio
so that minority examples are repeated more often, and
\textbf{ORB}~\cite{cabral2019orb} further boosts the resampling rate of the
class the model is currently biased against. In all three, $\lambda$ depends on
the \emph{number} of defect labels and not on their correctness, so a false
positive is amplified exactly as much as a true one. This observation
motivates Phase~3.

\subsection{Model complexity and simple baselines}

Deep models such as DeepJIT~\cite{hoang2019deepjit} and
CC2Vec~\cite{hoang2020cc2vec} reported large gains, but Zeng et
al.~\cite{zeng2021lapredict} found that \textbf{LApredict}, a logistic
regression on the added-lines feature alone, outperformed both, and
\textbf{JITLine}~\cite{pornprasit2021jitline}, a simpler feature-based model,
was more accurate and much faster than either. We use LApredict and JITLine
because they sit at opposite ends of model capacity.

\subsection{Learning with noisy labels}

Natarajan et al.~\cite{natarajan2013noisy} study class-conditional label noise
--- the noise model Phase~1 measures --- and derive an unbiased loss that
becomes unstable as $\rhoz+\rhoo \to 1$. Co-teaching~\cite{han2018coteaching}
trains two networks that exchange small-loss examples, and Confident
Learning~\cite{northcutt2021confident} estimates the joint distribution of
noisy and true labels to prune suspect examples. All three assume a fixed
dataset, repeated passes and labels that are wrong but stable, whereas a
JIT-SDP stream gives a single pass and labels that arrive late and may later
be corrected.

\subsection{Research gap}

SZZ evaluation research measures label noise but stops at the labels. Online
JIT-SDP research handles latency and imbalance but treats label noise as a
nuisance rather than a measured quantity. Noisy-label learning assumes a noise
model instead of estimating one, in a batch setting. \textbf{No existing work
measures the downstream cost of SZZ-origin noise --- asymmetric,
variant-dependent, with known flip rates --- under latency-aware online
evaluation.} What makes this measurable here is an independently constructed
reference label set, held out from training and used only for scoring.

\subsection{The reference labels}\label{sec:reference}

The reference labels come from JIT-Defects4J and were built in two stages.
Lines inside bug-fixing commits were annotated manually and kept only where at
least three annotators agreed that the line fixes the bug; \texttt{git blame}
was then applied to those verified lines to find the introducing commits, and
all other commits are clean. We confirmed this provenance against the
dataset's replication package, matching the published defect counts project
by project.

The reference is therefore \textbf{\texttt{git blame} seeded with
human-verified fix lines}, not manually verified ground truth. Three
consequences follow. (i)~Comparing an SZZ variant with it isolates the effect
of \emph{tangling}, since both sides use the same blame step and differ only
in which fix lines seed it. (ii)~Blame attribution error is present on both
sides and cancels, so \textbf{all measured noise is a lower bound}.
(iii)~Every reference positive is reachable by blame, so no measured false
negative can be blamed on blame being unable to trace a defect.

\subsection{Features and preprocessing}

Each commit is described by the 14 Kamei change-level metrics
(Table~\ref{tab:kamei}), used as published rather than recomputed so that
feature extraction cannot differ between label sources. Commits are
de-duplicated on (project, commit hash) and sorted by author timestamp, which
fixes the stream order for every temporal regime. The raw inputs (a 101\,MB
archive and 830\,MB of cloned repositories) were reduced to two small derived
files, so the whole study runs from committed inputs.

\subsection{Verification latency}

To model when a label would really become known, we extracted the author date
of every bug-fixing commit from the project repositories; a commit's label
arrives on the date of the fix that blamed it. As Figure~\ref{fig:latency}
shows, the median latency is 113 days and \textbf{53\% of defect labels arrive
after a 90-day window}. The published reference labels carry no
fix-to-inducing links, so their arrival times were reconstructed from the SZZ
mappings (coverage 67.8\%); the reference is therefore SZZ-derived in timing,
which is the main validity threat for the streaming results
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{f6_latency}
\caption{Verification latency (days from a commit to the fix that blames it)

Larger corpora such as ApacheJIT~\cite{keshavarz2022apachejit} and
Defectors~\cite{mahbub2023defectors} were not used because their labels are
SZZ-derived and so cannot serve as a reference for measuring SZZ noise.

paired comparison at the project level, and every number in this report is
regenerated from committed inputs by a named script.

\subsection{Phase 1: measuring label quality}

Six SZZ variants (Table~\ref{tab:variants}) were run with \textbf{PySZZ
v2}~\cite{rosa2023szzvariants}, the reference implementation released with the
developer-informed-oracle study, on the same set of bug-fixing commits. Each
variant's output was aligned to the reference over an \textbf{identical
27,319-commit denominator}, since a variant scored on a partial denominator can
look more precise simply by being evaluated on fewer commits. For each variant
we compute precision, recall, F1, MCC and Cohen's $\kappa$ against the
reference, and pairwise $\kappa$ between variants. Each variant is also
described as a class-conditional noisy channel between the reference label
$y$ and the variant label $\tilde{y}$:
\begin{equation}
\rhoz = P(\tilde{y}=1 \mid y=0) \;\;\text{(false-alarm rate)}, \qquad
\rhoo = P(\tilde{y}=0 \mid y=1) \;\;\text{(miss rate)}.
\end{equation}
The six $(\rhoz,\rhoo)$ pairs are saved as a reusable artefact for calibrating
the Phase~3 noise experiments.

\subsection{Phase 2: measuring downstream impact}

Phase~2 is a four-way factorial experiment (Table~\ref{tab:design}) over 21
projects and 10 random seeds, giving \textbf{16,380 evaluation records}.

\paragraph{Scoring convention.} This is the core of the design. A
\emph{self-scored} model is evaluated against the same SZZ labels it was
trained on, which is the usual practice in the literature. An
\emph{oracle-scored} model is trained on SZZ labels but evaluated against the
reference labels, which are never used for training. Self-scoring measures how
faithfully a model reproduces a heuristic; oracle-scoring measures how well it
finds real defects. The gap between the two is measurable only because the
reference is held out.

\paragraph{Latency protocol.} In the prequential regime each commit is
predicted on arrival at time $t$ and scored at once against the reference.
Its training label is released with a window $W = 90$ days: a clean label at
$t+W$; a defective label at the fix time if the fix falls within the window;
and if the fix comes later, \emph{first as a clean label} at $t+W$ and then
corrected at the fix time. Defective commits with no linked fix are delivered
as clean and never corrected. A learner under latency is therefore actively
trained on labels it will later discover were wrong, and these are exactly the
minority-class examples it most needs.

\subsection{Metrics and statistical protocol}\label{sec:metrics}

The primary metric is the \textbf{Matthews correlation
coefficient}~\cite{matthews1975mcc}, which uses all four cells of the confusion
matrix and is zero for any uninformed predictor:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MCC} = \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}
{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}.
\end{equation}
G-mean is reported alongside, and Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960kappa}
measures agreement between labelling procedures. In the streaming regime MCC
is a trajectory rather than one number. We use its \emph{time-averaged} value
as the primary summary and a \emph{terminal} value from a fading confusion
matrix (factor 0.99) as a secondary one. Neither is canonical, because MCC is
not decomposable; the time-averaged value is preferred because its
project-level variance is about half as large (0.051 against 0.092).

Every test is \textbf{paired at the project level} ($n=21$, seeds averaged
before pairing), since pooling the 16,380 records would be pseudo-replication.
Each comparison reports the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, the
matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation ($+1$ means every project moved in the
same direction), the Hodges--Lehmann (HL) estimate~\cite{hodges1963} and a
bootstrap 95\% confidence interval for it. Phase~2 runs 74 tests, corrected
with Holm's method~\cite{holm1979} both within each test family and
\textbf{globally across all 74}; headline claims rest on the global
correction. Phases~1 and~2 are exploratory and were not pre-registered.

\subsubsection{Per-variant quality}

Table~\ref{tab:p1} and Figure~\ref{fig:p1} show three findings.
\textbf{(1)~A precision ceiling of 27.2\%:} between 72.8\% and 81.5\% of the
commits any variant flags are not defect-introducing according to the
reference. \textbf{(2)~Asymmetric noise that differs by variant:} B-SZZ is
false-positive-heavy, flagging 8,060 commits to catch 1,495 real ones
($\rhoz = 0.263$), while L-SZZ and R-SZZ are the mirror image, with few false
alarms but 73.3\% and 70.0\% of real defects missed. Modelling SZZ noise as
symmetric random flips, the usual assumption in noisy-label learning,
therefore misrepresents it. \textbf{(3)~No variant dominates:} MCC ranks
B-SZZ first and RA-SZZ, the most refined variant, last; $\kappa$ ranks L-SZZ
first; F1 compresses all six into 0.259--0.288.

Rates and volumes also differ: B-SZZ's $\rhoz = 0.263$ yields 6,565 false
positives, while L-SZZ's larger $\rhoo = 0.733$ yields only 1,710 false
negatives, because the positive class is small. Per-project spread is large
(B-SZZ's recall ranges from 0.220 to 0.919), so all later tests are paired by
project.

The annotation-graph family (AG, MA, RA) agrees internally at $\kappa$

this corpus it is evidence of shared error.

\subsubsection{Where the false negatives come from}

Splitting each variant's false negatives against B-SZZ reveals two mechanisms
of similar size. \emph{Seed-line divergence} accounts for 47--63\%: SZZ and the
reference blame different lines of the same fix and land on different
commits. \emph{Self-inflicted filtering} accounts for 37--53\%: these are
defects that B-SZZ found correctly and the variant's own refinement then
discarded. For the two most conservative variants this is the larger share ---
R-SZZ and L-SZZ discard 55.2\% and 60.4\% of the reference defects that B-SZZ
had found. The refinements do cut false alarms ($\rhoz$ falls from 0.263 to
0.067), but more than half of what the most aggressive filters remove is
correct.

\subsubsection{Answer to RQ1}\label{sec:rq1}

SZZ variants disagree with the reference far more than with each other
($\kappa$ 0.158--0.202 against up to 0.933), and their bias is bifurcated in a
way that follows from their design: B-SZZ over-flags because it blames every
modified line, while R-SZZ and L-SZZ under-flag because they reduce the
candidate set to a single commit. \textbf{Refinement has moved error from the
false-positive column to the false-negative column rather than reducing it.}
Our precision values (0.18--0.27) are lower than those of Rosa et al.\ because
the denominators differ: they ask which earlier commit introduced a
\emph{known} defect, whereas we ask whether each of 27,319 commits, most of
them clean, is defect-introducing.

\subsubsection{Result 1: temporal leakage inflates in proportion to capacity}

Moving from random $k$-fold to a chronological split, JITLine on reference
labels loses \textbf{0.137 MCC} (HL $+0.137$, 95\% CI $[+0.105, +0.172]$,
rank-biserial $+1.000$, global Holm $p = 7.1\times10^{-5}$): all 21 projects
without exception. LApredict loses only 0.034, which does not survive
correction. A one-feature model has little capacity to exploit leakage, while a
100-tree forest has a great deal, so the apparent superiority of high-capacity
models over simple baselines is partly an artefact of the protocol that
measures it.

\subsubsection{Result 2: self-scoring inflates further, depending on the model}

Scoring the same predictions against the training labels rather than the
reference produces \textbf{the largest effect in the study}: $+0.230$ MCC for
JITLine on B-SZZ (CI $[+0.189, +0.278]$, global Holm $p = 1.0\times10^{-4}$).
A random forest trained on B-SZZ appears about two and a half times better
than it is, because it is graded by the heuristic that taught it. Under
JITLine the gap is significant for all six variants ($+0.18$ to $+0.23$ for
the four most permissive ones); under LApredict it is significant only for
B-SZZ ($+0.155$) and is negative for R-SZZ (Figure~\ref{fig:self}). This
capacity dependence is what one would expect if a model fits the error
structure of its labels, and we tested it directly: among the 8,060 commits
B-SZZ flags, a random forest separates its false positives from its true
positives with mean AUC 0.599, against 0.514 with permuted labels (HL
$+0.088$, 17 of 21 projects, $p = 0.0016$). SZZ's errors are learnable,
although only modestly.

\subsubsection{Supplementary analyses}\label{sec:supp}

\paragraph{Sizing the latency effect.} Read directly off the ladder, latency
would account for about 9\% of the batch-to-stream drop, but that contrast is
not identified, because the learner, the label timing and the evaluation
window all change together. A $2\times2$ factorial that separates the learner
(frozen or adaptive) from the labels (immediate or delayed) estimates a
latency effect of about $+0.023$ and an adaptivity effect of about $-0.046$,
but \textbf{every contrast, including the interaction, has a confidence
interval spanning zero}. With 21 projects, effects below roughly 0.04 MCC
cannot be resolved, so this is reported as unresolved rather than null; a
design that holds the learner fixed is needed (Phase~3).

\paragraph{The JITLine anomaly.} Under the chronological split, JITLine trained
on the noisier B-SZZ labels (0.132) beats JITLine trained on reference labels
(0.102) in 15 of 21 projects. The effect is genuine rather than seed noise
(97.7\% of the between-project variance is real, and it survives four
threshold-selection protocols), but none of the nine project characteristics
tested --- including defect count, B-SZZ precision and recall, and flag rate
--- predicts where it occurs. It is also batch-only: under streaming, the
reference beats B-SZZ in 18 of 21 projects. We report it as a robust
observation without a claimed mechanism.

\subsubsection{Robustness}

The label-source result was re-run over 20 settings of fading factor
(0.90--0.999) and warm-up length (0--25\%) for all six variants: in
\textbf{120 of 120} comparisons the HL estimate favours the reference, the
interval excludes zero and the result survives grid-wide Holm correction.
Excluding the eight projects with fewer than 25 test-half defects moves no
headline estimate by more than 0.002 MCC. One secondary result weakens: the
self-scoring gap under the chronological regime falls from 0.098 to 0.075 and
loses significance.

\subsubsection{Answer to RQ2}

The label source shifts measured performance significantly under every regime
that permits a fair test: under realistic streaming, training on SZZ instead
of the reference costs 0.034--0.064 MCC, between a third and two-thirds of the
available signal (0.097). \textbf{The evaluation protocol shifts it far more.}
Random $k$-fold inflates JITLine by 0.137 and self-scoring adds a further
0.230; together the protocol effects are about nine times the label-source
effect, and both grow with model capacity. The two problems are linked,
because self-scoring is possible only when the labels are heuristic.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Summary of findings}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SZZ labels are far noisier than usually assumed.} No variant
exceeds 27.2\% precision against the reference, and the noise is
class-conditional: B-SZZ is false-positive-heavy, L-SZZ and R-SZZ
false-negative-heavy.
\item \textbf{Agreement between SZZ variants is not evidence of correctness}:
$\kappa$ up to 0.933 with one another, but only 0.158--0.202 with the
reference.
\item \textbf{Refinement relocates error rather than reducing it}: more than
half of what the most aggressive filters remove is a correctly found defect.
\item \textbf{The evaluation protocol outweighs the label choice}: temporal
leakage (+0.137) and self-scoring (+0.230) together are about nine times the
label-source effect (0.034--0.064), and both grow with model capacity.
\item \textbf{Most reported performance is a measurement artefact}: 0.41 MCC
under the common protocol is 0.10 under a realistic one. Better labels alone
would not make JIT-SDP strong, but the field's reported numbers overstate its
current capability roughly four-fold.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to validity}\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Construct:} the reference is \texttt{git blame} seeded with verified
fix lines rather than ground truth, so all measured noise is a lower bound;
and its label arrival times are SZZ-derived (coverage 67.8\%), which is the
most serious threat to the streaming results. \textbf{Internal:} the corpus is
SZZ-shaped, since the dataset excludes changes that add no lines, so defects
introduced purely by deletion cannot appear. \textbf{Conclusion:} with 21
paired projects, effects below about 0.04 MCC are not resolvable, and the
analyses are exploratory, which is why headline claims rest on global Holm
correction. \textbf{External:} one benchmark family of 21 Apache Java
projects.

\subsection{Planned work}\label{sec:planned}

\paragraph{Phase 3 --- mechanism (RQ3).} Phase~2 shows that label quality
matters, but not whether the cost comes from the false positives SZZ adds or
from the defects it misses; comparing label sources cannot settle this,
because each source differs in many ways at once. Phase~3 will therefore
intervene: it will inject noise calibrated to the Phase~1 flip rates into the
reference labels, and separately repair real B-SZZ labels one error type at a
time, keeping the stream, learner and latency model fixed. Phase~2 dictates
three controls: a \emph{matched-mass} condition, because B-SZZ has about eight
false positives for every false negative; an \emph{immediate-delivery}
condition, so that repaired labels are not lost to latency; and a true
\emph{no-latency} arm in which every label arrives at once. ORB's internal
oversampling rate will also be recorded, to test whether its amplification of
defect labels --- driven by their number, not their correctness --- is the
route by which noise does damage.

\paragraph{Phase 4 --- mitigation (RQ4).} Guided by the Phase~3 findings,
Phase~4 will design a noise-aware online learner that does not require clean
labels. It will be \textbf{pre-registered} --- hypotheses, primary model,
acceptance criteria and multiplicity correction committed before the first
run --- and its acceptance bar will include a \emph{non-degradation} check,
since a method that helps on noisy labels but hurts on clean ones cannot be
adopted by a practitioner who does not know which case applies.
